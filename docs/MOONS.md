## Io

### Notable
- Jupiter's moon Io is the most volcanically active world in the solar system, with hundreds of volcanoes, some erupting lava fountains dozens of miles (or kilometers) high. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/io/)
- Io is caught in a tug-of-war between Jupiter's massive gravity and the smaller but precisely timed pulls from two neighboring moons that orbit farther from Jupiter—Europa and Ganymede. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/io/)
- Only slightly larger than Earth’s Moon, Io is about one-quarter the diameter of Earth itself. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/io/)

### Surface and make-up
- Volcanic activity on this Jovian moon is so abundant, it resurfaces the face of Io with new deposits faster than comets and asteroids can mark it with large craters. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/io/) [page lede]
- Io’s volcanoes are at times so powerful that they are seen with large telescopes on Earth, and Io even has lakes of molten silicate lava on its surface. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/io/)

### Visits
- Most of what we know about Io comes from the Galileo spacecraft, which made a detailed study of the Jupiter system. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/io/)
- It was Voyager 1 that spotted the first signs of the little moon’s powerful volcanism in 1979. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/io/)
- Since 2003, three spacecraft – Ulysses, Cassini, New Horizons, and Juno – have made additional close observations of Io. Juno is currently orbiting Jupiter, but its prime mission is focused on the giant planet, not the moons. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/io/)
- Jupiter's turbulent moon, Io, captured during a close approach by NASA's Juno spacecraft on Dec. 30, 2023. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/io/) [image caption]

## Europa

### Notable
- There is strong evidence Jupiter's moon Europa has a saltwater ocean that may be one of the best places to look for environments where life could exist beyond Earth. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/europa/) [page lede]
- Europa may be one of the most promising places in our solar system to find present-day environments suitable for some form of life beyond Earth. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/europa/)
- Europa is the fourth largest of Jupiter’s moons. It's the sixth-closest moon to the planet. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/europa/)

### Surface and make-up
- The surface of Jupiter's icy moon Europa looms large in this color view, made from images taken by NASA's Galileo spacecraft in the late 1990s. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/europa/) [image caption]

### Discovery and name
nothing found on this page

### Visits
- NASA's Europa Clipper launched on Oct. 14, 2024, on the first mission to conduct a detailed science investigation of Europa. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/europa/)
- NASA launched Europa Clipper on Oct. 14, 2024, to determine whether there are places below Europa's surface that could support life. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/europa/)
- Six robotic spacecraft have explored Europa, and scientists do regular observations of the moon with Hubble Space Telescope. Europa was first observed up close during the Jupiter flybys of Pioneer 10, Pioneer 11, Voyager 1 and Voyager 2. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/europa/)
- Most of what we know about the moon comes from observations by the Galileo mission. NASA's Juno spacecraft is orbiting Jupiter, and has made close flybys of Europa. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/europa/)
- This view of Jupiter's icy moon Europa was captured by JunoCam, the public engagement camera aboard NASA's Juno spacecraft, during the mission's close flyby on Sept. 29, 2022. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/europa/) [image caption]

## Ganymede

### Notable
- Jupiter’s icy moon Ganymede is the largest moon in our solar system, even bigger than the planet Mercury, and the dwarf planet Pluto. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/ganymede/)
- Ganymede is the only moon known to have its own magnetic field – something typically found on planets like Earth. The magnetic field causes auroras, or bright ribbons of glowing gas, that circle the moon’s poles. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/ganymede/)
- There’s strong evidence that Ganymede has an underground saltwater ocean that may hold more water than all the water on Earth's surface. It might even have ice and oceans stacked up in several layers like a club sandwich. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/ganymede/)
- The largest moon in the solar system, this icy world is the only moon to have its own magnetic field – and the atmospheric auroras that come with that. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/ganymede/) [page lede]

### Surface and make-up
- The dark areas are the older, more heavily cratered regions and the light areas are younger, tectonically deformed regions. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/ganymede/) [image details]
- The brownish-gray color is due to mixtures of rocky materials and ice. Bright spots are geologically recent impact craters and their ejecta. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/ganymede/) [image details]
- Deep inside Ganymede, there’s a metallic iron core that generates the moon's magnetic field. A shell of rock surrounds the core, and another icy shell surrounds the rock. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/ganymede/)
- Images taken by Juno on June 7, 2021, provided a fresh look at features on Ganymede’s surface including craters, clearly distinct dark, and bright terrain, and long structural features possibly linked to tectonic faults. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/ganymede/)
- There is evidence Ganymede has a tenuous oxygen atmosphere. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/ganymede/)

### Visits
- Pioneer 10, NASA's first mission to the outer planets, had its closest encounter with Jupiter on Dec. 3, 1973. It was the first spacecraft to obtain close-up images of Jupiter, and three of its largest moons: Ganymede, Callisto, and Europa. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/ganymede/) [image details]
- Two decades later, the Galileo spacecraft captured this view during its first encounter with Ganymede, on June 26, 1996. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/ganymede/) [image details]
- Several NASA probes have explored Jupiter and its moons, including Ganymede. The Juno spacecraft was most recent, taking detailed photographs of Ganymede in June 2021. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/ganymede/)

## Callisto

### Notable
- Callisto is the most heavily cratered object in our solar system. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/callisto/) [page lede]
- Callisto is Jupiter’s second largest moon and the third largest moon in our solar system. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/callisto/)

### Surface and make-up
- Data from NASA’s Galileo spacecraft in the 1990s revealed Callisto may have a secret: a salty ocean beneath its surface. That finding put the once seemingly dead moon on the list of worlds that could possibly harbor life. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/callisto/)

### Discovery and name
- Callisto and Jupiter’s three other largest moons were discovered in 1610 by Italian scientist Galileo Galilei. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/callisto/)

### Visits
- Almost 400 years later, a spacecraft bearing his name—the Galileo orbiter—began the first in depth study of the Jovian system, including Callisto and its sister moons. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/callisto/)
- Galileo orbited Jupiter until the mission ended in 2003. Since then, several NASA spacecraft, including Cassini and New Horizons, have studied the moon. (https://science.nasa.gov/jupiter/jupiter-moons/callisto/)
